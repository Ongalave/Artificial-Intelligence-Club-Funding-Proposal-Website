## 1. What are the largest, best-evidenced opportunities, and what quantitative estimates exist (with caveats)?

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced upside is a **trade-facilitation-led efficiency gain**, not a tariff-cut gain. The World Bank (2020) puts full implementation at about +7% regional real income (~$450bn) by 2035, with roughly two-thirds from facilitation. Realised intra-African trade is rising (about $214bn in 2025) but stays near 15-17% of Africa's trade, far below Europe, Asia and the Americas. The sector-specific opportunities (payments, aviation, power, battery minerals, autos/textiles) are real but early-stage, and most evidence for them is announcements or pilot-scale, not outcomes.

### Cited Findings

**A. Headline modelling estimates (with vintage)**
- **World Bank (July 2020 press release; report "The AfCFTA: Economic and Distributional Effects")**: full implementation could raise regional income by 7% (nearly $450bn) and lift ~30 million people out of extreme poverty by 2035. The agreement would connect 1.3 billion people across 55 countries with combined GDP of $3.4 trillion. — [World Bank press release, 27 Jul 2020](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2020/07/27/african-continental-free-trade-area); summaries in [IDN Financials](https://www.idnfinancials.com/archive/news/35436/World-Bank-African-trade-pact-could-boost-regional-income-by-US450-billion), [GNA](https://gna.org.gh/2020/07/free-trade-pact-could-boost-africas-income-by-450-billion-report/), [BusinessDay NG](https://businessday.ng/lead-story/article/africas-free-trade-pact-can-boost-regional-income-by-450bn-says-world-bank/)
- **World Bank (2020) decomposition**: $292bn of the $450bn comes from trade-facilitation measures (less red tape, lower compliance costs, easier integration into global supply chains). Tariff liberalisation plus reduction of non-tariff barriers adds about 2.4% of income (~$153bn). Note: one summary describes the non-tariff element as "quotas and rules of origin"; I could not confirm the exact scope of the 2.4% component. — [IDN Financials](https://www.idnfinancials.com/archive/news/35436/World-Bank-African-trade-pact-could-boost-regional-income-by-US450-billion); [Indrastra](https://www.indrastra.com/2020/07/AfCFTA-World-Bank-006-07-2020-0035.html); [World Bank press release](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2020/07/27/african-continental-free-trade-area)

Tariff-revenue loss about $4.1bn (about 9.1% of current tariff revenues). **E. Single African Air Transport Market (SAATM)**
- Launched January 2018. Malawi became the 38th country to join in February 2025; 26 "implementation states" have signed the Memorandum of Implementation as of 2026 (as stated in a search summary of an ICAO session deck). 113 new intra-African routes in 2022-26, 19 under fifth-freedom rights; fifth-freedom traffic rose from 15% (2018) to 23% (2024), target 30% by 2027. Projected benefits of full implementation: +50% connectivity, up to 26% lower travel/cargo cost, ~155,000 jobs, $4.6bn GDP (projection, not outcome). IATA welcomed SAATM but warned implementation is the test. — [ICAO session deck, Mar 2026](https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/2026-03/Session-6.1_Advancing-Africa-s-Air-Transport_Vasco-Vieira_V2.pdf); [Air Cargo Week](https://aircargoweek.com/iata-welcomes-saatm-warns-implementation-key/); [The Reporter Ethiopia](https://www.thereporterethiopia.com/7434/)

**F. Energy trade / power pools**
- Five regional power pools exist (Southern, Eastern, Western, Central, North). SAPP is the most advanced, yet only 7.7 TWh was traded in 2023 against 344 TWh demand (about 2%). WAPP growth is constrained by incomplete links, regulatory fragmentation and payment arrears; EAPP by regulatory fragmentation, infrastructure gaps and security tensions (African Energy Chamber, State of African Energy 2026 Outlook). — [Energy Chamber](https://energychamber.org/african-power-pools-how-regional-integration-can-strengthen-energy-security/); [Engineering News, 23 Jun 2026](https://www.engineeringnews.co.za/article/regional-power-pools-africas-greatest-opportunity-to-accelerate-electrification-2026-06-23)

**G. Industrial value chains: critical minerals/batteries, autos, textiles**
- **DRC-Zambia battery value chain**: 2022 cooperation agreement for a transboundary SEZ to make battery precursors rather than export raw ore; joint precursor plant targeting ~100,000 t/yr of NMC 622 material. DRC cabinet approved the Musompo SEZ (900 ha, Lualaba) as a priority in February 2026: >$200m cost, targeting ~$2bn private investment. In October 2025, under Egypt's presidency, member states reached consensus on the pending clothing/textile and automotive rules via a **transitional implementation mechanism** with a roadmap to finalise rules after a transition, after >4 years of talks. By March 2026 the focus moved to making rules usable and uniformly administered by customs. — [Financial Afrik, 6 Sep 2025](https://www.financialafrik.com/en/2025/09/06/afcfta-rules-of-origin-facing-obstacles-in-the-automotive-and-textile-sectors/); [Arab Finance](https://arabfinance.com/en/news/newdetails/Egypt-secures-African-consensus-on-outstanding-AfCFTA-rules-of-origin); [Bilaterals.org](https://bilaterals.org/egypt-manages-to-reach-african); [AfCFTA Secretariat, Sep 2025 (SG calls for political guidance on RoO)](https://au-afcfta.org/2025/09/afcfta-secretary-general-calls-for-political-guidance-on-rules-of-origin-at-4th-ministerial-retreat/); [Freight News](https://freightnews.co.za/article/next-steps-after-rules-of-origin-agreements-concluded)

### Inferences
- (Inference) About 65% of the World Bank's $450bn ($292bn/$450bn, my arithmetic) is contingent on **facilitation reforms inside national customs, border and standards agencies**, not on negotiated tariff schedules. The "opportunity" and the "obstacle" are therefore the same variable: state capacity and the incentives of border officials.
- (Inference) The IMF's loss estimate (0.03-0.22% of GDP) set against gains concentrated in facilitation implies tariff schedules are the politically loud but economically second-order part of the deal. Distribution is the real risk (see Q2/Q3).
- (Inference) PAPSS: if "1,000%" means about 11x volume while value rose about 2.2x, average ticket size fell to roughly one-fifth of the prior year. That suggests growth in small/retail/SME payments, not large trade settlements, off a very small base. Treat as hypothesis; the base year values were not found.
- (Inference) The DRC cobalt ban/quota shows that battery value-chain ambitions are shaped by resource-holder bargaining and unilateral export controls, not by AfCFTA rules. A Zambia-DRC SEZ is a bilateral anchor deal, a possible template for "variable geometry".
- (Inference) The spending projections (Brookings $6.7tn by 2030) are pre-2021 vintage and rest on growth that the 2025-26 shocks (aid cuts, Middle East war price effects per IMF) may have dented; do not present them as current.
- (Inference) Power-pool and SAPP data (2% of demand traded) indicate that energy integration is physically and financially (arrears) limited; it is a long-run opportunity, not a near-term gain.

## 2. What are the largest obstacles, which are binding versus lesser frictions, and what evidence ranks them?

### Takeaway
Evidence points to **non-tariff barriers/trade facilitation, infrastructure, thin and similar productive structures, and payments/currency frictions** as the continent-wide binding constraints, with tariff-revenue loss binding only for a narrow set of small, revenue-dependent states (SACU's BLNS, Togo-type economies, small LDCs). In 2025-26 external and fiscal shocks (aid collapse, debt distress, US tariffs, CBAM, Sahel exit from ECOWAS, xenophobia crises) add stress that reduces governments' tolerance for adjustment costs.

### Cited Findings

**A. Tariff revenue and fiscal dependence**
- **IMF (2020)**: revenue loss 0.03%-0.22% of GDP; IMF empirical work suggests fiscal losses are "limited". — [IMF SDN](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/Staff-Discussion-Notes/Issues/2020/05/13/The-African-Continental-Free-Trade-Area-Potential-Economic-Impact-and-Challenges-46235)
- **UNECA/UNCTAD (c. 2018-19)**: about $4.1bn (about 9.1% of tariff revenue) lost continent-wide; the burden is uneven. — [UNECA](https://archive.uneca.org/node/16065); [UNCTAD](https://unctad.org/publication/african-continental-free-trade-area-challenges-and-opportunities-tariff-reductions)
- **Country distribution (World Bank-style findings, attribution approximate)**: for most countries customs-revenue losses after tariff phase-down are minor (<0.2% of total government revenue), but smaller economies (Malawi, Liberia, Central African Republic, Sierra Leone) face high shares of total revenue; DRC, Cameroon, Republic of Congo and Zimbabwe are vulnerable to large dollar and share declines. Many countries insulated themselves via exclusion lists (Schedule C) and back-loaded cuts on revenue-sensitive goods (Schedule B). — [UCT PRISM Working Paper 2023-5 (Edwards)](https://commerce.uct.ac.za/sites/default/files/media/documents/commerce_uct_ac_za/869/PRISM%20Working%20Paper%202023-5_Edwards.pdf); [Togo case, JUTA Journals](https://www.jutajournals.co.za/impact-of-the-afcfta-agreement-on-customs-revenue-case-of-togo)
- **Togo case study**: customs revenue loss in the first three years of implementation estimated at CFA 30.9bn, equal to 0.25% of GDP and 22.67% of customs revenue. — [JUTA Journals](https://www.jutajournals.co.za/impact-of-the-afcfta-agreement-on-customs-revenue-case-of-togo)
- **SACU dependence**: shared SACU revenue provides 28-40% of total government revenue for Botswana, Namibia, Eswatini and Lesotho; Namibia's SACU receipts were budgeted at 32.6% of FY2023/24 revenue; Eswatini and Lesotho have received about 40% of revenue from SACU since 2010. South Africa supplies about 97% of the pool; BLNS members receive three to five times their proportionate shares. — [The Brief Namibia, Dec 2023](https://thebrief.com.na/2023/12/namibia-rakes-in-n110billion-in-revenue-from-sacu/); [The Brief Namibia, Feb 2023 (over-reliance warning)](https://thebrief.com.na/2023/02/namibia-warned-over-sacu-revenue-over-reliance/); [ODI, "National budget trends in SACU: struggling for control"](https://odi.org/en/insights/national-budget-trends-in-the-southern-african-customs-union-struggling-for-control/)
- **Distribution of gains/losses**: an ECOWAS simulation (WTO Chairs programme) found larger and/or previously closed economies lose more than smaller, already-open ones (Ghana, Mali, Nigeria, Senegal, Togo named among larger losers in that simulation); gains vary by size, export diversification, infrastructure and tariff-revenue loss. — [WTO Chairs: "Who wins and who loses under the AfCFTA? ECOWAS simulation"](https://wtochairs.org/node/4986); [Atlantic Council](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/?p=109066)
- **Country headlines (underlying studies not read)**: "Kenya faces Sh22.53bn revenue loss under full FTA implementation" and "South Africa could lose $4.5bn under new African trade deal" (Al Jazeera, Feb 2020). — [Eastleigh Voice](https://eastleighvoice.co.ke/african%20continental%20free%20trade%20area/69279/kenya-faces-sh22-53bn-revenue-loss-under-full-free-trade-area-implementation); [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/economy/2020/2/28/south-africa-could-lose-4-5bn-under-new-african-trade-deal)

**B. Non-tariff barriers, trade facilitation, rules of origin**

### Inferences
- **Proposed ranking (researcher inference; ranks are judgments built on the documented points above):**
  1. *Binding, continent-wide*: NTBs/trade facilitation and customs administration (largest share of modelled gains; persistent in EAC and ASEAN); logistics/infrastructure (AfDB gap $68-108bn a year); shallow, similar, commodity-heavy production structures (low intra-African complementarity); payments/FX frictions (partly addressable by PAPSS, but dollar-routed).
  2. *Binding for specific coalitions/countries*: tariff-revenue dependence (SACU's BLNS; Togo-like economies; small LDCs such as Malawi, Liberia, CAR, Sierra Leone per the cited study); debt distress and aid cuts, which shrink the fiscal space needed to absorb adjustment and to fund compensation.
  3. *Amplifiers/exogenous*: US tariff regime and AGOA uncertainty; China zero-tariff (reduces relative value of intra-African preferences for commodity exporters); CBAM (hits Mozambique, South Africa, Egypt, Morocco; raises the value of diversification but also divides exporters); EU-Mercosur and similar deals (competing preference regimes).
  4. *Lesser friction*: tariff levels per se (IMF/World Bank modelling shows small aggregate effect).
- (Inference) Export similarity is an obstacle to **trade volume** but also a political problem: commodity exporters see little gain from preferences while import-competing manufacturers see risk; the winners (South Africa, Egypt, Morocco, Kenya) are few. This amplifies distributive conflict.
- (Inference) Debt distress and aid cuts raise the political cost of any revenue loss and make governments less willing to accept even small tariff-revenue declines; the IMF's "limited" aggregate loss may understate the salience at country level, especially for small, distressed states.
- (Inference) Several documented episodes (Nigeria 2019, Tanzania-Kenya, Zambia-DRC) show that border closures, import bans and ad hoc duties are used as bargaining or domestic-political tools and are not resolved by treaty text; recourse is bilateral/ministerial.
- (Inference) The China zero-tariff scheme (all products, 53 countries) is a larger preference than AfCFTA gives any African exporter in African markets; it could bias exporters toward China for raw/semi-processed goods and, by raising imports from China to Africa, intensify manufacturers' protectionist demands (I did not find data on Chinese imports displacing intra-African trade).

## 3. Why do these challenges matter politically (domestic coalitions, sovereignty, regime security, regional hegemony concerns) and economically (revenue, jobs, value chains)?

### Takeaway
The obstacles are not neutral technical frictions: each maps onto an identifiable domestic interest (customs agencies and border officials, revenue ministries, import-competing manufacturers, cotton versus garment producers, small hosts of duty-dependent budgets, informal traders) and onto regime-security and sovereignty concerns (border control, resource nationalism, anti-migrant politics). Regional hegemons (South Africa, Nigeria, Egypt, Kenya) are both the main potential winners and the targets of suspicion.

### Inferences
- (Inference) **Who gains/loses domestically.** Likely winners: scaled exporters and manufacturers in autos, processed food, textiles (Egypt, Morocco, South Africa, Ethiopia, Kenya in the cited evidence), logistics and payment firms, importing consumers, and, if formalisation is real, women/SME traders. Likely losers or resisters: import-competing manufacturers and farmers (rice, sugar, confectionery cases), revenue authorities and finance ministries (customs revenue, especially in small/LDC states), customs/border staff whose rents depend on discretion, cotton growers in RoO bargaining, and small states dependent on hegemon-run revenue pools.
- (Inference) **Sovereignty/regime security.** Border closures, export controls and migration politics are also regime-legitimacy tools. Governments with narrow security or fiscal margins (Sahel juntas, debt-distressed states) discount long-term integration gains. The AES exit suggests that a regional grouping can lose members when integration conditions collide with regime-security preferences.
- (Inference) **Hegemony.** Because intra-African trade is concentrated in South Africa, Egypt/Morocco and a few others, small states read "openness" as exposure to a few exporters, while the hegemons fear being asked to finance compensation (the SACU pattern). The Nigeria 2019 and South Africa 2026 episodes show hegemons are also not reliable champions of openness when domestic politics turn.
- (Inference) **Why the politics bind the economics.** Since ~65% of modelled gains require administrative reform, and the actors who must implement those reforms (customs, border and standards agencies) partly lose from them, the benefits are diffuse and delayed while costs are concentrated and early. That asymmetry predicts slow, selective implementation even if heads of state endorse the project.
- (Inference) **Jobs and value chains.** Concern for jobs in vulnerable sectors (Lesotho apparel; Mozambique aluminium exposed to CBAM) shows why governments prioritise external-market access (AGOA, EU) over intra-African access when forced to choose; this redirects political capital away from AfCFTA implementation.

## 4. How might these constraints affect governments' capacity to agree on future implementation priorities, and what mechanisms can bridge them?

### Takeaway
Documented evidence shows the AfCFTA system **progresses by deadline-driven, consensus-seeking packages with built-in flexibility** (transitional rules of origin, tariff schedules B/C, a pilot-then-conclude Guided Trade Initiative, an Adjustment Fund). Ratification of Phase II/III protocols is the visible bottleneck. The February 2026 AU decisions (a new Heads-of-State implementation committee, a new Protocol on Industrial Policy and Development, an end-2026 ratification target) show where the next priority fights will be, and where side-payments can be bundled.

### Cited Findings
- **Ratification status**: 54 of 55 AU members have signed (Eritrea has not). 50 had ratified after Somalia's ratification on 20 October 2025; Benin, Libya, South Sudan and Sudan are reported as signed but not ratified. Conflicting snapshot: another summary says 49 had deposited instruments as of July 2026 and 50 as of 4 September 2026. Treat as "about 49-50 parties" and verify. — [AfCFTA ratification search summary / trade.gov Ghana update, Dec 2025](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/ghana-african-continental-free-trade-area-afcfta-december-2025-update); [tralac ratification status](https://www.tralac.org/resources/infographic/13795-status-of-afcfta-ratification.html); [Africanews, 2019 (earlier outsiders)](https://www.africanews.com/2019/05/31/eritrea-nigeria-benin-outsiders-from-africa-free-trade-zone-afcfta/)
- **Phase II / III protocols adopted but not in force**: Investment, Competition Policy and Intellectual Property Rights adopted by the AU Assembly in February 2023; Digital Trade and Women and Youth in Trade adopted afterwards (one summary says February 2024, another says February 2025 as "Phase III" with eight Digital Trade annexes; classification conflicts). None is yet in force; the Digital Trade Protocol needs 22 ratifications. — [tralac, "Must the Phase II protocols be ratified to be implemented?"](https://www.tralac.org/publications/article/17080-must-the-phase-ii-afcfta-protocols-be-ratified-to-be-implemented.html); [tralac legal texts](https://www.tralac.org/resources/by-region/cfta.html); [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/international-trade-investment/1816936/one-africa-one-click-what-the-afcfta-digital-trade-protocol-means-for-africa)
- **February 2026 AU Assembly (39th Ordinary Session, Addis Ababa, 14-15 Feb)**: inaugurated the Assembly Committee of Heads of State and Government on Implementation of the AfCFTA (a High-Level Implementation Committee chaired by Kenya's President William Ruto, per summaries); directed the Council of Ministers to **initiate negotiations on a Protocol on Industrial Policy and Development**; approved eight of nine annexes to the IP Protocol; asked the committee to mobilise ratification of the Investment, Competition, IPR, Digital Trade, and Women and Youth protocols **by end-2026**, with annual progress reports from 2027. — [AU Assembly 39th session page](https://au.int/en/newsevents/20260214/39th-ordinary-session-assembly); [tralac, AU Assembly decision on the report of the 1st Committee meeting, Feb 2026](https://www.tralac.org/documents/resources/cfta/7495-au-assembly-decision-on-report-of-1st-meeting-of-assembly-committee-of-heads-of-state-and-government-implementation-afcfta-february-2026.html); [Guardian NG](https://guardian.ng/business-services/afcfta-secretariat-all-africa-intellectual-property-summit/); [UCT Trade Talks Africa, Mar 2026](https://commerce.uct.ac.za/school-public-governance/articles/2026-03-12-clone-trade-talks-africa-policy-progress-and-people-behind-afcfta-issue-8); [Decode39](https://decode39.com/13522/african-union-summit-highlights-a-continent-under-pressure-seeking-to-shape-the-global-agenda/)
- **Rules-of-origin precedent for compromise**: SG called for political guidance at the 4th Ministerial Retreat (September 2025); then a consensus was reached in October 2025 via a transitional mechanism. — [AfCFTA Secretariat](https://au-afcfta.org/2025/09/afcfta-secretary-general-calls-for-political-guidance-on-rules-of-origin-at-4th-ministerial-retreat/); [Arab Finance](https://arabfinance.com/en/news/newdetails/Egypt-secures-African-consensus-on-outstanding-AfCFTA-rules-of-origin)
- **Existing flexibilities**: Schedules B/C (back-loaded and excluded revenue-sensitive products) already used by many states (World Bank-style finding; attribution approximate). — [UCT PRISM Working Paper](https://commerce.uct.ac.za/sites/default/files/media/documents/commerce_uct_ac_za/869/PRISM%20Working%20Paper%202023-5_Edwards.pdf)
- **Guided Trade Initiative**: conclusion confirmed at the 16th Council of Ministers (April 2025), described as having met its objectives. — [tralac](https://www.tralac.org/resources/by-region/cfta.html)
- **AfCFTA Adjustment Fund**: Afreximbank's Board approved $1bn plus a $10m grant for set-up; structure of a Base Fund (state contributions, grants, technical assistance for tariff-revenue losses and implementation), a General Fund and a Credit Fund; manager is FEDA (Kigali), target $8-10bn. — [AfCFTA Secretariat page](https://au-afcfta.org/?p=7197); [Bilaterals.org](https://bilaterals.org/ethiopia-among-afcfta-1bln); [Nairametrics](https://nairametrics.com/?p=464807); [The Reporter Ethiopia](https://www.thereporterethiopia.com/?p=31427)
- **REC fragility as a baseline for continental talks**: the Sahel states' exit from ECOWAS shows member-state divergence can break a REC that AfCFTA is meant to build on. — [Amani Africa](https://amaniafrica-et.org/the-withdr)

### Inferences
- (Inference) **Pattern**: the system resolves stalemates through (a) deadlines and high-level political guidance, (b) transitional/phase-in compromises (RoO October 2025), (c) letting willing states go first (GTI), and (d) side-funds. Expect the same toolkit to be applied to the next fights: investment/competition protocols (investor protection and state-aid sovereignty), digital trade (data/regulatory sovereignty; Nigeria/South Africa/Kenya vs smaller markets), industrial policy (who gets to subsidise), and eventually dispute settlement and any customs union.
- (Inference) **Why ratification lags.** Phase II/III protocols require national legislatures to accept constraints (investment rules, data flows) for gains that are uncertain; with fiscal stress and anti-integration pressures, ministries have little incentive to spend political capital. A 22-ratification threshold for entry into force is achievable in principle (50 states have ratified the base agreement), but the end-2026 target looks tight (this is judgement, not documented; no ratification count for the protocols was found).
- (Inference) **Bridging mechanisms most plausible for 2026-30** (documented building blocks marked *):
  1. *Compensation/adjustment funding*: Adjustment Fund* with a targeted eligibility rule (small revenue-dependent states) rather than a continent-wide entitlement; the IMF's low aggregate loss suggests the compensation need is concentrated and potentially affordable, but I could not find evidence of the Fund's actual capitalisation or disbursements.
  2. *Longer phase-ins and sensitive/excluded lists*: Schedules B/C* and RoO transition mechanism* (precedent exists).
  3. *Variable geometry / coalitions of the willing*: GTI*, regional anchor deals such as DRC-Zambia battery SEZ* and power-pool corridors. Formal legal status of variable geometry in AfCFTA text was not verified.
  4. *Special and differential treatment for LDCs and small states*: the UNCTAD recommendation for flanking measures and flexibilities*.
  5. *Packaging*: bundling the new industrial-policy protocol* with revenue/adjustment support and RoO could trade industrialisers' support for compensation for fiscally dependent states.
  6. *Facilitation-first sequencing*: since facilitation gains dominate, priorities that deliver visible border-time and cost cuts (single windows, mutual recognition, anti-bribery enforcement) can unite most governments, while contested protocols follow.
- (Inference) **Enforcement gap**: I found no sourced detail on AfCFTA dispute settlement operation or on any supranational enforcement; the border closures and ad hoc bans documented in Q2 were all handled through bilateral/ministerial channels, implying that compliance relies on peer pressure and politics. This raises the importance of credible dispute-settlement and compliance monitoring in any Phase-next agenda.
- (Inference) **External shocks as both brake and spur.** US tariff unpredictability and AGOA's one-year renewal may push some states to hedge toward intra-African markets; China's zero tariffs and EU-Mercosur-style deals offer alternatives. Which effect dominates is not established.

### Gaps
- Decision-making rules (consensus versus two-thirds majority at the AU Assembly/Council; AfCFTA Council of Ministers, Committee of Senior Trade Officials) were **not sourced** this session. ## 5. What does comparative experience (EU, ASEAN, Mercosur, SACU, EAC, WAEMU, USMCA) suggest about sequencing and compensation?

### Takeaway
Across blocs, **tariff removal comes first and is easiest; non-tariff measures, regulatory convergence and exceptions persist; and bargains hold only when compensation or asymmetry is built into the design (SACU's formula, EAC's asymmetric tariff phase-out, EU-style transfers)**. Customs unions tolerate exception lists and strain when members cut deals with outside powers (Mercosur-US/Argentina; USMCA annual reviews). The best-sourced lessons for AfCFTA are therefore about NTMs, compensation, and managing external pressure.

### Cited Findings
- **EU single market (lessons paper, J. of African Trade 2021, "Proving Hegel Wrong")**: from the 1993 Single Market Programme the main lessons for AfCFTA are the scale of benefits to be expected, the role of multinational corporations, the need for effective competition policy, and public buy-in; but structurally (development level, resources to support integration) the EU case differs sharply from Africa's. — [Journal of African Trade](https://atlantis-press.com/journals/jat/125961185); [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.2991/jat.k.210908.001); [The Reporter Ethiopia, "AfCFTA's lessons from the EU"](https://www.thereporterethiopia.com/10713/)
- **ASEAN/AEC**: intra-ASEAN trade rose only from 23% (2000) to 25.3% (2014) of members' total trade despite tariff cuts; NTMs became the main remaining hurdle; of 69 NTMs identified, 45 had been resolved (as reported in a Philippines government institute summary and trade-press); regulatory convergence could reduce NTM costs by 15-25%. — [PIDS, Philippines](https://www.pids.gov.ph/details/increasing-nontariff-measures-limit-ph-capacity-to-trade-in-asean-region-pids-study); [Antidumping.vn](https://antidumping.vn/regional-pact-eyes-further-elimination-of-nontariff-barriers-n13282.html); [JRC](https://publications.jrc.ec.europa.eu/repository/handle/JRC125378)
- **Mercosur**: customs-union exception lists have been expanded: the number of items Brazil and Argentina may place on the CET exception list rises from 100 to 150 by 2028 (Uruguay 225 to 275 by 2029; Paraguay 649 to 699 by 2030); Argentina sought temporary extra exemptions amid global trade tensions; Brazilian officials reportedly examined a US-Argentina trade deal (said to cover closer to 200 items) for conflict with Mercosur rules. EU-Mercosur interim agreement provisionally applied from 1 May 2026. — [Datamar News](https://datamarnews.com/?p=62415); [AJOT/Reuters](https://www.ajot.com/news/brazil-examines-us-argentina-trade-deal-over-mercosur-conflicts-sources-say); [PIIE, 2026](https://www.piie.com/blogs/realtime-economics/2026/mercosur-risk-us-tariffs-and-politics); [Finnish Customs](https://tulli.fi/en/-/free-trade-agreement-between-the-eu-and-mercosur-countries-to-be-applied-provisionally-from-1-may-2026-note-the-rules-of-origin)
- **USMCA**: at the first six-year joint review (1 July 2026) the US declined to extend the agreement in its current form, triggering annual reviews through 2036; the agreement stays in force with preferences unchanged. Rules of origin are central: US priorities include a higher automotive regional value content (reported 82% RVC / 50% US-content proposal) and rules to check Chinese transshipment through Mexico; Mexico seeks relief from 50% steel/aluminium and 25% auto Section 232 tariffs. Further US-Mexico bilateral rounds continue (fourth round scheduled for September 2026). — [White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/usmca-2026-joint-review-united-states-declines-extend-agreement-triggering-annual); [Greenberg Traurig, Jul 2026](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2026/7/usmca-joint-review-triggers-annual-reviews-but-does-not-alter-current-trade-benefits); [ICPA tracker](https://www.icpainc.org/usmca-joint-review-rules-of-origin/)
- **SACU**: Common Revenue Pool; formula has a customs component (linked to intra-SACU trade), an excise component (largely GDP shares) and a development component (more for less-developed members). Revised substantively in 1969 and during 1994-2002 (the 2002 formula raised BLNS shares and removed South Africa as residual claimant). BLNS members receive 3-5 times their proportional contribution and depend on the pool for 28-40% of revenue; South Africa pays about 97% of the pool and pushes for review; one source says BLNS argue their share is fair given South Africa's de facto control of regional tariff policy. — [SACU factsheet](https://www.sacu.int/uploads/documents/SACU%20FACTSHEET%20UNDERSTANDING%20THE%20SACU%20REVENUE%20SHARING%20ARRANGEMENT%205_9-211024-095719.pdf); [Africa Portal, "SACU Revenue Sharing Formula: towards a developmental agreement"](https://africaportal.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/SACU_Revenue_Sharing_Formula_-_Towards_a_Developmental_Agreement.pdf); [Africa Portal, "The SACU revenue formula: history of an equation"](https://africaportal.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/TheSACURevenueFormula-TheHistoryofAnEquation.pdf); [DNA Economics](https://dnaeconomics.com/?p=19349); [BusinessDay, Jul 2025](https://www.businessday.co.za/bd/opinion/2025-07-01-henni-britz-time-to-review-sacus-revenue-sharing-formula/)
- **EAC customs union (2005)**: asymmetric tariff liberalisation was designed to give Uganda and Tanzania time to adjust (Kenya eliminated internal tariffs at once in 2005, while its goods still faced tariffs into the others until 31 December 2009). Non-tariff barriers (weighbridges, roadblocks, excise/levy measures, standards) have persisted since; Tanzania is cited as the most frequent source; EAC analysis finds the common external tariff effect dominated intra-EAC liberalisation effects. — [Capital FM, "A fully fledged Customs Union in place"](https://capitalfm.africa/a-fully-fledged-custom-union-in-place/); [EAC repository](https://repository.eac.int/items/5aad956a-2c59-42a0-aab5-d7707a566f41); [MPRA paper 80961](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/80961/1/MPRA_paper_80961.pdf); [The EastAfrican](https://theeastafrican.co.ke/tea/business-tech/tanzania-blamed-for-39pc-of-eac-trade-barriers-5575306)
- **WAEMU/UEMOA and ECOWAS CET**: WAEMU adopted a four-band CET (0/5/10/20%) in 1997, in force 1 January 2000, with some national divergences remaining; ECOWAS heads of state adopted a similar four-band tariff in January 2006 (non-WAEMU states to transition by end-2008, which did not happen on time), and the ECOWAS CET took about 10 years to negotiate among 15 members and was formally adopted in October 2013 (the snippets do not describe the final band structure); Nigeria began implementing in 2014, six months before the planned 1 January 2015 start. ECOWAS directors-general of customs reviewed implementation in 2019 and proposed corrective measures for adversely affected sectors. — [FERDI note on adapting to the ECOWAS CET](https://ferdi.fr/dl/df-b4ZPYtVWGy14VkWXVkgkZsZg/ferdi-b107-reflections-on-adapting-to-the-ecowas-cet.pdf); [Bilaterals.org](https://bilaterals.org/ecowas-common-external-tariff); [ECOWAS](https://old22.ecowas.int/?p=28932); [Nairametrics](https://nairametrics.com/?p=63575)
- **Earlier Tripartite FTA modelling**: Mold & Mukwaya found modest concentration risk and positive manufacturing effects (see Q1). — [Journal of African Trade](https://atlantis-press.com/journals/jat/125905673)

### Inferences
- (Inference) **Sequencing lesson**: ASEAN and EAC both show tariffs are the easy part and NTMs the lasting obstacle. For AfCFTA, an implementation priority list that puts NTB elimination mechanisms (reporting-and-resolution systems, regulatory/standards convergence) ahead of deeper tariff or customs-union ambition is better supported by the evidence than the reverse.
- (Inference) **Customs-union caution**: WAEMU's CET worked in a small monetary-union core; ECOWAS took ~10 years for 15 members; Mercosur tolerates growing exception lists and strain from outside deals. A continental customs union across 54-55 states and eight RECs would face far greater divergence, so any near-term step is more plausibly REC-level convergence or a "customs-union-lite" (common standards, mutual recognition, joint customs data) than a single external tariff. That follows my reading of the evidence, not a documented prediction.
- (Inference) **Compensation lesson**: SACU shows a revenue formula can sustain a customs union for over a century, but creates dependence (28-40% of revenue), volatility, and resentment by the payer, and is only workable when a dominant member is willing to subsidise. A continental version would lack a single willing payer. The Adjustment Fund model (external capital plus state contributions) is therefore the more plausible route, with targeting to small/LDC revenue-dependent states.
- (Inference) **Asymmetry lesson (EAC)**: front-loading cuts by the larger economy (Kenya) with delayed reciprocal cuts by smaller ones is a template for differentiated phase-ins that could address large/small imbalances; the persistence of NTBs afterwards shows asymmetry does not substitute for NTB enforcement.
- (Inference) **EU lesson**: the EU programme relied on competition policy, multinationals and public buy-in, and on resources absent in Africa; the AfCFTA's equivalents (Phase II competition/investment protocols; fiscal transfers) are weaker and not yet in force.
- (Inference) **USMCA/Mercosur lesson**: rules of origin and external-tariff policy become instruments of great-power or hegemon leverage when the bloc contains a dominant market; for Africa this suggests careful design of RoO (as in the October 2025 transitional mechanism) and guarding against bilateral side deals (US, EU, China) that pre-empt continental positions.
