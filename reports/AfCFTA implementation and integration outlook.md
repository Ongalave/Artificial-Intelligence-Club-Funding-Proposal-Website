# Africa's free trade pact outruns its implementation

As of October 2026 the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) is a finished legal framework that has not yet visibly changed how Africa trades. Fifty of the 55 African Union members are State Parties (Somalia deposited in early September 2026) and rules of origin are reported complete, with interim terms for autos and textiles, **but only about 24 to 25 countries have gazetted tariff schedules and can trade on preferences.** Intra-African trade was about US$214 billion in 2025, roughly 15% of Africa's trade, and Afreximbank's downward revision of its 2024 figure by about US$17.6 billion is larger than the 2025 increase of about US$11 billion, so no AfCFTA effect can yet be shown in the aggregate data. The regional blocs the AfCFTA is built on have a similar record: strong on instruments (ECOWAS's common external tariff, SADC's 2008 free trade area, the EAC's customs union) and weak on enforcement, with intra-bloc export shares running from about 2% (ECCAS) to about 20% (SADC, EAC), and with ECOWAS and the EAC now strained by the Sahel exit and by more than US$89 million of unpaid EAC dues. The World Bank's best-known estimate, a 7% real-income gain (about US$450 billion) by 2035, draws about two-thirds of its value from trade facilitation rather than tariffs, which makes customs, logistics, payments and national administrative capacity the binding obstacles. Governments agree on "implementation"; they split over who absorbs adjustment costs, whether treaty rules should bind industrial policy, how to sequence deeper integration, and whether to hedge through AGOA and China. My judgment is that facilitation-first sequencing is the defensible priority and that the end-2026 target for ratifying five Phase II protocols will be missed. ## Forty years of planning produced a 50-state treaty and a stalled trade share

The AfCFTA is the third generation of a continental-market idea. The 1980 Lagos Plan of Action described Africa as entering the decade as the "least developed region in the world", declared an Industrial Development Decade and aimed to reduce dependence on Western economies ([OAU Lagos Plan text](https://www.resakss.org/sites/default/files/OAU%201980%20Lagos%20Plan%20of%20Action%20for%20the%20Economic%20Development%20of%20Africa.pdf)). The 1991 Abuja Treaty, in force since 12 May 1994, then set six stages to an African Economic Community by 2028 with the regional economic communities (RECs) as building blocks ([AU working document](https://www.au.int/web/sites/default/files/newsevents/workingdocuments/31409-wd-fast_tracking_the_cfta_-_recs_as_building_blocks_-_presentation_by_prudence.pdf)). The AU Assembly decided in 2012 to create a continental free trade area ([Globalex](https://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/afcfta.html)); leaders signed in Kigali on 21 March 2018, with 44 of 55 members signing on the day ([tralac](https://www.tralac.org/news/article/12865-10th-extraordinary-session-of-the-assembly-of-the-african-union-on-afcfta-held-in-kigali.html)); the agreement entered into force on 30 May 2019, thirty days after the 22nd ratification arrived on 29 April ([Globalex](https://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/afcfta.html)); the operational phase was launched at the AU's Niger summit in 2019 ([tralac](https://www.tralac.org/news/article/14142-operational-phase-of-the-african-continental-free-trade-area-launched-at-niger-summit-of-the-african-union.html)); and preferential trading began on 1 January 2021 ([Nairametrics](https://nairametrics.com/2026/09/08/afcfta-at-five-the-3-4-trillion-market-africa-still-wont-trade-in/)). The nineteen months between entry into force and trading foreshadow what follows: the legal act arrives first and the administrative capacity to use it second. Several often-repeated details (the Agenda 2063 linkage, the 2015 launch of negotiations, individual leaders' roles) could not be confirmed from retrievable pages, so I leave them out.

Legally the AfCFTA is a framework agreement plus protocols. Phase I, in force, covers trade in goods, trade in services and dispute settlement. Investment, competition policy and intellectual property were concluded in October 2022 and adopted by the Assembly on 18-19 February 2023 ([tralac FAQs](https://www.tralac.org/publications/article/13784-afcfta-questions-and-answers.html)); the Digital Trade and Women and Youth in Trade protocols followed in February 2024 ([Covington](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2024/02/africa-technology-regulatory-update-adoption-of-the-afcfta-protocol-on-digital-trade); [UNECA](https://www.uneca.org/stories/blog-the-afcfta-protocol-on-women-and-youth-in-trade-%E2%80%93-africa%E2%80%99s-blueprint-for-inclusive)), with eight digital-trade annexes added in February 2025 ([UCT Trade Talks Africa](https://commerce.uct.ac.za/school-public-governance/articles/2026-03-12-clone-trade-talks-africa-policy-progress-and-people-behind-afcfta-issue-8)). Sources disagree on whether the digital protocol belongs to "Phase II" or a "Phase III"; tralac files it under Phase II ([tralac FAQ](https://www.tralac.org/documents/resources/faqs/5889-afcfta-frequently-asked-questions-updated-may-2025/file.html)). Each of the five needs 22 ratifications plus 30 days to enter into force. My sources do not include the agreement's objectives clause, so its goals show up through its instruments and targets: a single market of 55 countries, an AU aim of lifting intra-African trade to 25% or more of the total ([tralac](https://www.tralac.org/resources/by-region/14352-african-union-agenda-2063.html)), and, on goods, a commitment to liberalise 90% of tariff lines over five years for non-LDCs and ten for LDCs, with up to 7% of lines treated as sensitive (ten and thirteen years) and up to 3% excluded, subject to a cap of 10% of intra-African import value ([South Centre](https://www.southcentre.int/wp-content/uploads/2019/06/PB63_Phase-1B-of-the-AfCFTA-negotiations_EN-1.pdf); [UN LDC Portal](https://www.un.org/ldcportal/content/african-continental-free-trade-area-agreement-what-expected-ldcs-terms-trade-liberalisation)). A "G6" (Ethiopia, Madagascar, Malawi, Sudan, Zambia, Zimbabwe) negotiated 85% over ten years plus a further 5% over fifteen ([UNECA](https://archive.uneca.org/sites/default/files/PublicationFiles/brief_assessment_of_afcfta_modalities_eng_nov18.pdf)); one summary adds Djibouti, which I could not confirm. Sensitive-list cuts were reported to start on 1 January 2026 ([Malawi Nation](https://mwnation.com/tariff-delays-stall-mws-afcfta-trade/)), so the longest phase-downs are only now beginning.

Fifty State Parties is the correct headline, but the dating needs care. Eritrea alone has not signed; Benin, Libya, South Sudan and Sudan have signed without ratifying; Somalia became the 50th State Party by depositing at AU headquarters in early September 2026 ([Rio Times](https://www.riotimesonline.com/afcfta-status-2026/); [Dawan Africa](https://www.dawan.africa/news/somalia-ratifies-afcfta-becomes-50th-member-and-eyes-made-in-somalia-exports)). Sources give the deposit as 2 September or 4 September. One summary puts Somalia's ratification on 20 October 2025, which matches its parliamentary approval that month ([African Newspage](https://www.africannewspage.net/2025/10/somali-parliament-ratifies-afcfta-agreement/)) rather than a deposit, and fits a January 2026 count of 49 ([Habari Network](https://www.thehabarinetwork.com/afcfta-january-2026-update-progress-and-tough-road-ahead)); I treat 20 October 2025 as approval, which is an inference. Nairametrics, writing on 8 September, still called Somalia's paperwork incomplete, which I attribute to lag.

The trade data show why "status" needs care. Afreximbank's 2026 African Trade Report puts intra-African trade at US$213.8 billion in 2025, up 5.47% from US$202.7 billion in 2024, about 15% of Africa's roughly US$1.5 trillion merchandise trade ([Guardian Nigeria](https://guardian.ng/business-services/intra-african-trade-rises-by-5-5-as-regional-integration-gains-momentum/); [Nairametrics](https://nairametrics.com/2026/06/25/afreximbank-intra-african-trade-rises-5-5-to-213-8-billion/)). Its 2025 report had given 2024 as US$220.3 billion, up 12.4% after a 2023 contraction, with a 15.3% share ([Afreximbank](https://www.afreximbank.com/afreximbank-launches-2025-report-on-african-trade-in-a-shifting-global-financial-landscape/)). The revision of US$17.6 billion, about 8%, exceeds the entire 2025 gain of about US$11 billion, and 2025's level now sits below the originally published 2024 level; a third outlet reports US$206.6 billion without a clear vintage ([Economist, Namibia](https://economist.com.na/intra-african-trade-rises-to-us206-6-billion-afreximbank-report/)). Growth rates from the two editions cannot be chained. Official projections inherit the problem: Secretary-General Wamkele Mene projects about US$250 billion in 2026 "up from about US$220 billion in 2025" ([BusinessDay Nigeria](https://businessday.ng/news/article/afcfta-targets-250bn-intra-african-trade-lauds-lagos-on-industrial-digital-transformation/)), a base the latest Afreximbank edition does not support, while an Ecofin report on UNECA figures expects 10% growth to about US$230 billion ([Ecofin](https://www.ecofinagency.com/news/1104-54591-intra-african-trade-set-to-grow-10-in-2026-as-afcfta-implementation-accelerates)); the two forecasts are unreconciled.

On a different measure, UNCTAD data show 16.9% of Africa's merchandise exports going to other African countries in 2024, against 12.1% in 1995, a 2015 peak of 18.8%, and 65.6% in Europe, 58.4% in Asia and 54.0% in the Americas ([Intelpoint, summarising UNCTAD](https://intelpoint.co/insights/africa-has-never-sold-more-than-20-of-its-goods-exports-within-the-continent-in-three-decades/)). Neither measure shows a break since 2021. In UNECA's composition figures (vintage not stated) manufactures are 46% of intra-African exports, food 21%, fuels 20% and ores and metals 7% ([UNECA](https://www.uneca.org/eca-events/stories/afcfta-benefits-will-be-across-sectors-economic-report-africa-2025)), but 2025's growth came from identifiable commodity flows, notably Nigerian refined-petroleum exports to Cameroon, Ghana and Togo and gains from the DRC and Zambia ([allAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202606230318.html)). Market-size headlines are equally soft: the "US$3.4 trillion" figure comes from the World Bank's 2020 report ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2020/07/27/african-continental-free-trade-area)), an IMF-based ranking implies about US$2.8 trillion nominal for Africa in 2025 ([Business Today Egypt](https://www.businesstodayegypt.com/amp/1/6266/Egypt-ranks-as-second-largest-economy-in-Africa-by-GDP)), and the IMF's own 2020 note used about US$2.2 trillion ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/Staff-Discussion-Notes/Issues/2020/05/13/The-African-Continental-Free-Trade-Area-Potential-Economic-Impact-and-Challenges-46235)).

The modelled gains are long-horizon scenarios, and the decomposition matters more than the headline. The World Bank's 2020 study finds that full implementation raises real income by 7% (nearly US$450 billion, 2014 prices) by 2035 and lifts 30 million people out of extreme poverty ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2020/07/27/african-continental-free-trade-area)), with unskilled wages up 10.3% and skilled up 9.8% ([tralac](https://www.tralac.org/news/article/14808-afcfta-trade-pact-could-boost-africa-s-income-by-450-billion-study-finds.html)). Tariff cuts alone add about 0.2%, tariffs plus non-tariff-barrier reduction about 2.4% (US$153 billion), and the Trade Facilitation Agreement lifts the total to 7% ([World Bank report text](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/216831595998182418/txt/The-African-Continental-Free-Trade-Area-Economic-and-Distributional-Effects.txt)); the remaining US$292 billion, about 65% by my arithmetic, depends on customs and border reform rather than negotiated schedules. UNECA's 2025 modelling is more modest, at +45% intra-African trade and +1.2% GDP by 2045 ([UNECA](https://www.uneca.org/eca-events/stories/afcfta-benefits-will-be-across-sectors-economic-report-africa-2025)); UNCTAD's 2019 estimate was about +33% once tariffs are fully liberalised ([Engineering News](https://m.engineeringnews.co.za/article/africa-needs-to-give-priority-to-rules-of-origin-for-products-unctad-2019-07-12)). The older 52.3% figure is a tariff-only partial estimate and is not comparable with the World Bank number ([African Arguments](https://africanarguments.org/2019/06/the-afcfta-is-laudable-but-its-imminent-benefits-are-overstated/)), and I could not reconcile an April 2026 report attributing "40 million" people lifted from extreme poverty to the World Bank ([Citi Newsroom](https://citinewsroom.com/2026/04/afcfta-could-lift-40m-africans-from-extreme-poverty-world-bank/)) with the 30 million in the 2020 report. I treat all of these as upper-bound scenarios rather than forecasts, on the assumption that they require full tariff phase-down and facilitation reform, which 2021-2025 implementation does not yet supply.

## Regional blocs missed most deadlines, and intra-African exports never passed 19%

Abuja scheduled the sequence: RECs strengthened in 1994-1999; tariffs and non-tariff barriers removed within RECs by 2007; free trade areas and customs unions in every REC by 2017; a continental customs union and common external tariff in 2018-19; a common market in 2020-23; and a monetary union with a central bank and single currency in 2024-28 ([AU working document](https://www.au.int/web/sites/default/files/newsevents/workingdocuments/31409-wd-fast_tracking_the_cfta_-_recs_as_building_blocks_-_presentation_by_prudence.pdf)). The Boosting Intra-African Trade Action Plan of January 2012 added seven clusters, from trade policy to factor-market integration, and a target of 25% or more of trade within a decade, up from 10-13% ([tralac](https://www.tralac.org/resources/by-region/14352-african-union-agenda-2063.html)). **Nearly every dated milestone has slipped.** SADC planned a customs union for 2010, missed 2013, and showed no progress by 2022, a delay attributed to Secretariat capacity ([Globalex](https://www.nyulawglobal.org/globalex/southern_african_development_community1.html)); COMESA planned its customs union for December 2004 and slipped over the level of the common external tariff ([COMESA in Brief](https://www.comesa.int/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/COMESA-in-brief-FINAL-_web.pdf)), and I found no current status. The continental customs union has not begun, and the AfCFTA, a free trade area with rules of origin, arrived in 2018-21 in its place. I read it as a re-sequencing of Abuja rather than its fulfilment on schedule; that is my synthesis of sourced stage and REC data. The institutions meant to crown the process are further behind: the African Monetary Fund protocol needs 15 ratifications and had none as of 2017, and the African Central Bank is pencilled in for 2028-2034 ([AU](https://au.int/en/financial-institutions)); the AU page's figures are 2017-dated and I found no current count.

The sourced rationale is consistent from 1980 to now: dependence on outside markets and narrow production. UNECA's integration assessments find that more than 80% of African exports leave the continent, that production is geared to primary commodities with little industrial capacity to supply diversified manufactures to regional markets, and that decades of REC experimentation did not yield significant intra-REC or intra-African trade ([UNECA ARIA IV](https://archive.uneca.org/node/488)). The Lagos Plan framed the same problem as a self-reliance programme. Other drivers one would expect (colonial-era borders and landlocked states, the 1980s debt crisis and structural adjustment, the EU as a model, the AU and NEPAD institutional turn) are not covered by any source I retrieved, so I do not rely on them. The 2025-26 shocks to US market access, aid and Chinese trade policy are a current driver and appear below.

The table draws on UNCTAD ([Facts and Figures](https://unctad.org/press-material/facts-figures-0)), tralac ([2021 update](https://www.tralac.org/resources/infographic/15772-intra-africa-trade-by-rec-2021-update.html)), the ECOWAS tariff page ([ECOWAS](http://www.aidfortrade.ecowas.int/programmes/ecowas-common-external-tariff-cet)), SADC and COMESA sources cited above, the EAC and monetary-union reporting below, and AMU and CEMAC sources ([Fanack](https://fanack.com/morocco/politics-of-morocco/the-arab-maghreb-union/); [Executive Magazine](https://www.executive-magazine.com/north-africa/trade-across-the-maghreb); [Africa Visa Openness](https://www.visaopenness.org/2025-visa-openness-remains-a-work-in-progress/regional-economic-communities/economic-community-of-central-african-states-eccas/)). IGAD and CEN-SAD, the other two AU-recognised RECs, and SACU and the Common Monetary Area were not researched; the only sourced IGAD fact is that a continental index rates it highest on regional infrastructure ([integrate-africa](https://www.integrate-africa.org/rankings/regional-economic-communities/)). The two share columns measure different things and overlap, so they cannot be summed or read as one ranking. SADC's 32% of all intra-African trade reflects South Africa's weight, while the EAC's 4.8% beside a 20.3% intra-bloc export share reflects small economies trading intensively with each other. Memberships overlap (Kenya and Uganda sit in both EAC and COMESA; Tanzania and, since 2022, the DRC in both EAC and SADC ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regional_economic_communities); [Chatham House](https://www.chathamhouse.org/2024/04/reviving-tanzanias-regional-leadership-and-global-engagement/03-between-eac-and-sadc))). A common currency does not explain the ranking either: ECOWAS, which contains WAEMU's CFA zone, sits at 10.6%, ECCAS, which contains CEMAC's, at 1.8%, and COMESA, with no currency union, at 10.2%. That is a coarse whole-bloc comparison, not a causal test.

**Thirty years of REC-led integration lifted the intra-African export share by about five points, and it has fallen since 2015.** UNCTAD's series runs 12.1% in 1995, 18.8% in 2015, 16.9% in 2024 ([Intelpoint](https://intelpoint.co/insights/africa-has-never-sold-more-than-20-of-its-goods-exports-within-the-continent-in-three-decades/)). Formal integration scores tell a kinder story. UNECA's ARIA XI (July 2025) calls progress "moderate but still incomplete", names the EAC, ECOWAS and SADC as top performers, and lists imperfect implementation of free trade areas and customs unions, overlapping memberships and weak institutions as the drags ([ARIA XI](https://www.uneca.org/sites/default/files/fullpublicationfiles/ARIA%20XI_Book_EN_12June_rev1.pdf); [DD Customs Law](https://ddcustomslaw.com/index.php?catid=29%3Ail-cms&id=1003%3Auneca-publishes-11th-edition-of-the-assessing-regional-integration-in-africa-aria-xi-report&lang=en&option=com_content&view=article)). The AU, UNECA and AfDB report a continental score of 0.62, highest on free movement (0.68) and trade (0.66), though the scale and edition are unclear in the summary ([AfDB](https://www.afdb.org/en/news-and-events/press-releases/africa-regional-integration-index-underlines-integration-key-building-more-resilient-economies-35742)). Secondary summaries disagree on which REC leads which dimension (one has ECOWAS first on free movement and IGAD on infrastructure, another has the EAC first on trade and free movement and SADC on infrastructure), and I did not reconcile them. The gap between scores and outcomes is sharpest at the EAC: it rates highest on integration indices, yet its legislature delayed salaries and suspended activities because partner states owe more than US$89 million against a US$109 million 2025/26 budget, with only Kenya and Tanzania paid up and the DRC (about US$27 million), Burundi (US$22.7 million), South Sudan (US$21.8 million) and Somalia (US$10.5 million) the largest debtors ([The Citizen](https://www.thecitizen.co.tz/tanzania/news/east-africa-news/eac-faces-cash-crunch-as-states-default-on-budget-contributions-5054090); [KT Press](https://www.ktpress.rw/2026/06/burundi-drc-top-eac-list-of-unpaid-contributions-as-bloc-moves-to-tighten-funding-rules/)). A new funding formula took effect on 1 July 2026, with half of dues paid equally and half by economic capacity, and presidents waived half the arrears if the rest is paid within two years of 7 March 2026 ([KT Press](https://www.ktpress.rw/2026/03/eac-adopts-new-funding-model-based-on-which-members-are-benefiting-the-most/)); one summary dates the formula's approval to March 2025, so the sequence needs checking. The EAC's Monetary Union Protocol (30 November 2013) aimed at a single currency by 2024, the date was moved to 2031, and no partner state meets all four convergence criteria ([The Citizen](https://www.thecitizen.co.tz/tanzania/business/why-delays-in-eac-s-quest-for-monetary-union-persist--5298858); [Garowe Online](https://www.garoweonline.com/en/featured/business-n/eac-states-to-wait-till-2031-for-a-regional-monetary-union)).

Two shocks show what paper integration is worth under stress. First, Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger left ECOWAS on 29 January 2025, a year after giving notice, and formed the Alliance of Sahel States (AES) ([Africanews](https://www.africanews.com/2025/01/29/ecowas-confirms-exit-of-the-alliance-of-sahel-states-from-the-regional-bloc)). ECOWAS chose a managed divorce, keeping tariff-free access and free movement for the three as temporary measures ([BusinessDay Nigeria](https://businessday.ng/africa/article/burkina-faso-mali-and-niger-exit-ecowas-as-regional-bloc-maintains-trade-travel-ties/)) and extending its negotiator's mandate to December 2026 ([WAVN](https://www.wavn.org/ecowas-extends-talks-with-sahel-states-until-end-of-2026/)); AES chair Ibrahim Traoré asked on 6 July 2026 for a "new framework" ([CapMad](https://capmad.com/post/on-aess-2nd-anniversary-ibrahim-traore-calls-for-a-new-framework-with-ecowas)). The AES meanwhile imposed a 0.5% levy from April 2025 on imports from outside the bloc, ECOWAS goods included ([allAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202504020030.html)), and one June 2026 assessment judged that it "has delivered a passport and an ECOWAS exit, but not a currency, and not security" while the three remain in the CFA franc zone ([Africa Index](https://africaindex.com/blog/aes-confederation-two-year-scorecard-june-2026)). ECOWAS reaffirmed in July 2026 an "eco" launch in 2027 with "those who are ready" and possibly without WAEMU ([Citi Newsroom](https://www.citinewsroom.com/2026/07/ecowas-reaffirms-plans-to-launch-single-currency-in-2027/); [Ecofin](https://www.ecofinagency.com/news-finances/1702-52963-ecowas-eco-currency-may-launch-without-waemu-in-2027-push)), and its biometric identity card, launched in seven states, aims for region-wide border acceptance by December 2026 ([Biometric Update](https://www.biometricupdate.com/202610/ecowas-pushes-biometric-id-toward-region-wide-border-acceptance-by-december)). I found no data on how the split changed trade volumes, port-transit flows or tariffs, so its cost is asserted but unquantified. Second, the DRC and Rwanda are both EAC members (the DRC also in SADC) and signed the Washington Peace Accords on 4 December 2025, yet the fifth Joint Security Coordination Mechanism meeting on 15-16 July 2026 did not declare the FDLR's neutralisation or Rwanda's lifting of "defensive measures" complete ([US State Department](https://www.state.gov/releases/office-of-the-spokesperson/2026/07/joint-statement-on-the-fifth-meeting-of-the-joint-security-coordination-mechanism-for-the-peace-agreement-between-the-democratic-republic-of-the-congo-and-the-republic-of-rwanda)), and a UN briefing in September 2026 said the deals had failed to halt fighting ([UN News](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/09/1168482)). Mediation sits with the United States, Qatar and an AU-appointed Togolese mediator, not with the EAC or SADC.

Continental instruments that cut across the RECs show the same pattern. The COMESA-EAC-SADC Tripartite Free Trade Area (29 states) entered into force on 25 July 2024 after a 14th ratification, with ratifiers holding about 75% of Tripartite GDP ([COMESA](https://www.comesa.int/comesa-eac-sadc-tripartite-free-trade-area-to-come-into-force-on-25th-july-2024/)). The AU Protocol on Free Movement of Persons has been signed by 32 countries and ratified by four (Mali, Niger, Rwanda, São Tomé and Príncipe) against 15 needed ([Africa Visa Openness](https://www.visaopenness.org/pages-not-in-use/latest-developments-on-the-afcfta/)); two of those four, Mali and Niger, are now AES states outside ECOWAS. Benin, The Gambia, Rwanda and Seychelles admit all African passport holders without a visa ([Africa Visa Openness](https://www.visaopenness.org/2025-visa-openness-remains-a-work-in-progress/the-african-continental-free-trade-area/au-protocol-on-the-free-movement-of-persons/)). A summary of the 2025 Africa Visa Openness Index reports the share of intra-African trips requiring a prior visa rising from 47.1% to 51.1%, a figure my sources themselves flag as unverified. The Single African Air Transport Market has 38 signatories (Malawi was the 38th, in February 2025), but a summary of an ICAO session deck says only 26 "implementation states" have signed the Memorandum of Implementation, though fifth-freedom traffic rose from 15% (2018) to 23% (2024) ([ICAO](https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/2026-03/Session-6.1_Advancing-Africa-s-Air-Transport_Vasco-Vieira_V2.pdf)).

## Only about 24 states trade on AfCFTA preferences, and the constraint is administrative

On paper the goods architecture is largely complete. Fifty tariff offers have been submitted, individually or by the customs unions CEMAC, EAC, ECOWAS and SACU, and 48 had been verified by May 2025, though only 17 states had complete Category A, B and C schedules by July 2025 ([tralac progress report](https://www.tralac.org/documents/resources/cfta/7851-progress-report-of-the-african-continental-free-trade-area-july-2025/file.html)); Libya, Sudan, Eritrea, Somalia, Djibouti and the Sahrawi Republic had submitted nothing ([Rio Times](https://www.riotimesonline.com/afcfta-status-2026/)). Services offers cover 49 members in five priority sectors (finance, communications, transport, tourism, business services), with 24 draft schedules adopted for provisional implementation ([AfCFTA Secretariat](https://au-afcfta.org/trade-in-services); [trade.gov](https://www.trade.gov/market-intelligence/ghana-afcfta-services-negotiations-update)), a count one source dates to February 2025. On rules of origin, about 92.43% of tariff lines had agreed rules by mid-2025, with automotive and clothing-and-textile rules outstanding ([tralac progress report](https://www.tralac.org/documents/resources/cfta/7851-progress-report-of-the-african-continental-free-trade-area-july-2025/file.html)). The 17th Council of Ministers in Cairo then agreed transitional rules (reports date this to September and October 2025), under which vehicles need at least 40% African content, up to 60% foreign inputs are tolerated, and the rule is reviewed after five years, and the Assembly signed off the automotive rule in February 2026 ([Engineering News](https://engineeringnews.co.za/article/major-step-forward-as-african-union-signs-off-on-made-in-africa-auto-trade-rules-aaam-2026-03-06)). The sources conflict on whether rules are now "100% agreed" (the South African dtic's March 2026 newsletter and Assembly-based reporting) or "92.4%" (a September 2026 Rio Times summary; AllBusiness.africa gives 92.3% and expected completion by February 2026) ([dtic](https://www.thedtic.gov.za/wp-content/uploads/Newsletter-on-the-AfCFTA-March-2026.pdf); [UCT Trade Talks Africa](https://commerce.uct.ac.za/school-public-governance/articles/2026-03-12-clone-trade-talks-africa-policy-progress-and-people-behind-afcfta-issue-8); [AllBusiness.africa](https://allbusiness.africa/insights/afcfta-implementation-2026)). **My reading is that every tariff line now has an agreed rule but that the automotive and textile rules are interim**; the 92.4% figure matches tralac's mid-2025 number and looks like stale re-reporting, an inference I could not check against the Secretariat's tracker.

**The binding statistic is not 50 ratifications but the number of states whose schedules are gazetted.** By January 2026 that was 24 ([Malawi Nation](https://mwnation.com/tariff-delays-stall-mws-afcfta-trade/)): Algeria, Botswana, Cameroon, Egypt, Ethiopia, Ghana, Kenya, Mozambique, Mauritius, Morocco, Namibia, Nigeria, Rwanda, Tanzania, Tunisia, South Africa, Eswatini, Lesotho, Burundi, Uganda, Malawi, Seychelles, The Gambia and Zambia in one secondary list ([AllBusiness.africa](https://allbusiness.africa/insights/afcfta-implementation-2026)). A South African government newsletter put the March 2026 figure at 25 "including SACU" ([dtic](https://www.thedtic.gov.za/wp-content/uploads/Newsletter-on-the-AfCFTA-March-2026.pdf)), earlier tralac counts were 23, and Ethiopia's regulation refers to 24 partners ([Capital Ethiopia](https://capitalethiopia.com/2025/11/09/ethiopia-implements-customs-tariff-reductions-with-24-afcfta-member-states-to-boost-continental-trade/)). The named list already counts all five SACU members, so the 25th is either SACU as a unit or an added state; I treat 24-25 as the estimate. The large economies arrived late. South Africa gazetted SACU's schedule on 26 January 2024 and began preferential trade on 31 January ([SARS](https://www.sars.gov.za/legal-lsec-ce-ta-2024-016-sch-1-1-1892-substitution-general-note-o-to-implement-the-afcfta-agreement-26-january-2024)); Nigeria gazetted the ECOWAS provisional schedule in April 2025 ([allAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202504150096.html)) and South Africa recognised it on 12 September 2025 ([dtic](https://www.thedtic.gov.za/wp-content/uploads/Newsletter-on-the-AfCFTA-March-2026.pdf)); Ethiopia began duty cuts against 24 partners in October 2025 ([Afriwise](https://www.afriwise.com/blog/ethiopia-implements-afcfta-tariff-concessions-council-of-ministers-regulation-no-574-2025)), with sources giving 9 and 24 October. Coverage is also bilateral: Malawi can trade preferentially with only about half of AfCFTA members because partners lack concessions or rules ([Malawi Nation](https://mwnation.com/tariff-delays-stall-mws-afcfta-trade/)). If each of 24 gazetted states extended preferences to every other, there would be 276 live country pairs of 1,431 possible, about 19% (my arithmetic; an upper bound, since pairs are not all mutually live).

The operational instruments are real but small. The Guided Trade Initiative, launched in Accra on 7 October 2022 with eight State Parties and 96 products ([Africa Renewal](https://africarenewal.un.org/en/magazine/afcftas-guided-trade-initiative-takes-set-ease-and-boost-intra-african-trade)), involved about 100 "potential trades" (Kenya's first shipment was US$77,000 of batteries to Ghana ([BFT Online](https://thebftonline.com/?p=127323))) and was declared concluded at the 16th Council of Ministers in April 2025 ([tralac](https://www.tralac.org/resources/by-region/cfta.html)); a figure of 39 participating countries circulates that I could not trace to a source. Certificates of origin reached 8,561 by June 2025 and, per the Secretariat, more than 12,000 by end-March 2026 ([AfCFTA Secretariat](https://au-afcfta.org/2026/07/afcfta-council-of-ministers-concludes-18th-meeting-in-abuja-nigeria-assumes-the-chairpersonship-as-focus-shifts-fully-to-implementation/)), though another summary says "more than 10,000". South Africa (4,658) and Egypt (2,852) account for about 62% of 12,000 if the figures share a date, which my sources do not establish ([AllBusiness.africa](https://allbusiness.africa/insights/afcfta-implementation-2026)). Certificates are counts, not values, and no source I retrieved measures how much of the roughly US$214 billion actually moves on preferences, so preference utilisation is unknown.

The online non-tariff-barrier mechanism has run since 13 January 2020; UNCTAD cites 583 resolved cases, undated ([UNCTAD](https://unctad.org/news/project-tackles-non-tariff-barriers-africa)), against over 600 reported and about 35% resolved by mid-2023 ([Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/international-trade-investment/1623526/non-tariff-barriers-under-the-african-continental-free-trade-area-challenges-and-opportunities-for-nigeria)). The Tripartite mechanism logged 928 complaints and resolved 803 over 2008-2025, while the EAC's average resolution time rose from 76 days in 2021 to 274 in 2024, against an AfCFTA target of 60 days for simple and 125 for complex cases ([TradeMark Africa](https://trademarkafrica.com/non-tariff-barriers-could-hurt-east-africas-integration-gains/)); regional mechanisms are not clearing barriers faster as the AfCFTA nears operation.

The Pan-African Payment and Settlement System (PAPSS) reports operating in about 30 countries, linking 24 central banks and roughly 200 financial institutions, with volumes up about 1,000% and values up about 120% between comparable 2025 and 2026 periods ([TechCabal](https://techcabal.com/2026/09/10/papss-payment-network-africans-use/); [TechAfrica News](https://techafricanews.com/2026/09/14/papss-records-1000-surge-in-transaction-volumes-as-african-network-expands/)). These are operator figures, and the gap between the two growth rates implies the average payment fell by about four-fifths (2.2 divided by 11, my arithmetic), consistent with growth from a small base in small tickets. The AfCFTA Adjustment Fund is a US$1 billion Afreximbank-run facility whose US$100 million credit window is reported operational with US$10 million disbursed ([allAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202603200071.html)), against an estimated adjustment need of about US$10 billion ([AfricanLII](https://africanlii.org/akn/aa-au/statement/statement/au-ec/2022/1143/eng@2022-02-03)).

Why preferences do not yet convert into trade is mostly a matter of costs that tariff schedules do not touch. The World Bank's August 2026 brief, "Integrating Africa: From Threads to Hubs", puts about 60% of Africa's trade costs behind the border (customs clearance, regulatory misalignment, weak logistics, fragmented transit) and observes that "broad coverage of policies is not matched by binding commitments, and binding commitments are not matched by the governance machinery needed to enforce them" ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/en/brief/2026/08/25/integrating-africa-from-threads-to-hubs)); a Nairametrics piece of 8 September conflates this with non-tariff barriers generally ([Nairametrics](https://nairametrics.com/2026/09/08/afcfta-at-five-the-3-4-trillion-market-africa-still-wont-trade-in/)). On customs and infrastructure, Lagos-Abidjan trucking can take up to 17 days over 1,080 km with up to 37 checkpoints ([Leadership Nigeria](https://leadership.ng/cargo-spends-17-days-on-lagos-abidjan-route-amid-37-checkpoints/)); early-2010s Doing Business data had Africa needing 31 days to export and 38 to import, at US$2,108 and US$2,793 per container, roughly 2.5 and 3.2 times East Asia ([World Bank](https://subnational.doingbusiness.org/content/dam/doingBusiness/media/Annual-Reports/English/DB13-Chapters/Trading-across-borders.pdf)), and I found no 2024-26 border benchmark. The AfDB's estimate of US$130-170 billion a year in infrastructure need and a US$68-108 billion gap is a 2018 figure still repeated, including by Mene ([BusinessDay Nigeria](https://businessday.ng/business-economy/article/africa-infrastructure-financing-gap/); [Shipping Position](https://shippingposition.com.ng/afcfta-implementation-hindered-by-ratification-delays-capacity-disparity-infrastructure-challenges-sec-gen/)). Firms are largely unaware: ITC reports over half of African micro, small and medium firms do not know of the AfCFTA ([Daily News Tanzania](https://dailynews.co.tz/itc-50pc-of-msmes-unaware-of-afcfta/)). Payments add friction, with 42 currencies, conversion costs Afreximbank puts at up to US$5 billion a year, and most intra-African payments clearing through dollars or euros ([Economic Confidential](https://economicconfidential.com/africa-currency-conversion-afcfta/)); the trade-finance gap is US$74 billion in the 2026 trade report ([Frontier Africa Reports](https://news.frontierafricareports.com/article/afreximbank-releases-2026-african-trade-report)) but about US$100 billion in another Afreximbank-based summary ([Guardian Nigeria](https://guardian.ng/business-services/intra-african-trade-rises-by-5-5-as-regional-integration-gains-momentum/)). On industrial development, 46 of Africa's 54 countries are highly commodity-dependent (UNCTAD, date unknown) ([VON](https://von.gov.ng/?p=151883)), which limits what preferences can carry. Mene himself cites capacity disparity, with some countries able to use the single market immediately and others lacking industrial capability ([Shipping Position](https://shippingposition.com.ng/afcfta-implementation-hindered-by-ratification-delays-capacity-disparity-infrastructure-challenges-sec-gen/)). Concentration reinforces the point: South Africa accounted for 19.2% of intra-African trade in 2025, the DRC 6.74% and Côte d'Ivoire 4.83% ([Guardian Nigeria](https://guardian.ng/business-services/intra-african-trade-rises-by-5-5-as-regional-integration-gains-momentum/)).

Political episodes show that continental commitments carry little weight against domestic politics. Nigeria closed land borders with Benin, Cameroon, Chad and Niger in August 2019 and reopened goods trade with Benin in December 2020 ([Africanews](https://www.africanews.com/amp/2020/12/17/nigeria-to-reopen-border-with-benin-for-goods-trade/)); ISS argued that the closures "haven't served their purpose" ([ISS](https://issafrica.org/iss-today/nigerias-border-closures-havent-served-their-purpose)), and analysts said the move ran against ECOWAS and AfCFTA commitments ([Brookings](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/nigerias-benin-border-closure-and-what-it-means-for-the-african-continental-free-trade-agreement-afcfta/)). The DRC banned Zambian soft drinks and beer in August 2024, Zambia shut its border in response, and the border reopened within a day ([Africanews](https://www.africanews.com/2024/08/13/zambia-and-congo-reopen-their-border-after-resolving-a-trade-dispute//)). The DRC banned cobalt exports in February 2025 and replaced the ban in October 2025 with quotas of 18,125 tonnes for the rest of 2025 and 96,600 tonnes a year from 2026 ([CRU Group](https://www.crugroup.com/en/communities/thought-leadership/2025/cobalt-curbs-beyond-the-drcs-export-ban-extension/)). In May 2026 anti-migrant mobilisation in South Africa led Ghana to petition the AU to add the issue to the mid-year meeting agenda and to evacuate about 300 nationals, while Nigeria repatriated about 1,500 and President Tinubu refused to meet a South African delegation ([News Ghana](https://www.newsghana.com.gh/ghana-asks-au-to-put-south-africa-xenophobia-on-summit-agenda/); [Briefly](https://briefly.co.za/world/africa/247549-xenophobia-row-ghana-nigeria-south-africa-au/)). Ghana's petition placed that meeting at 24-27 June; AU releases place the eighth Mid-Year Coordination Meeting at El-Alamein on 4 October 2026, with an agenda covering institutional reform, the AfCFTA and water security ([AU](https://au.int/en/pressreleases/20261004/8th-au-mycm-reaffirms-commitment-continental-integration-and-agenda-2063)), and I found no outcome on the xenophobia item. I found no evidence that the dispute-settlement mechanism, modelled on the WTO with panels and a seven-member Appellate Body ([Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/international-trade-investment/1373790/mauritius-and-the-afcfta-part-3-dispute-settlement)), has been used for any of these episodes, and no information on whether its panel roster or Appellate Body has been appointed. Security compounds the problem in the Sahel, eastern DRC and Sudan ([ISS PSC Report](https://issafrica.org/pscreport/psc-insights/africas-free-trade-initiative-could-bolster-continental-peace)); Cabo Delgado, Ethiopia and maritime piracy were not covered in my sources.

Institutional capacity is a real but second-order constraint. Phase 1 staffing was the Secretary-General, four directors and essential staff, and in 2022 the AU Executive Council asked that 104 earmarked posts be cut to what the budget could support ([AfricanLII](https://africanlii.org/akn/aa-au/statement/statement/au-ec/2022/1143/eng@2022-02-03)); funding comes mostly from AU member states, with the EU, GIZ and Denmark supplementing ([New Times Rwanda](https://www.newtimes.co.rw/business/afcfta-secretariat-gets-11m-grant-enhance-effective-implementation)) and Ghana, as host, committing US$10 million for set-up ([Graphic](https://www.graphic.com.gh/news/politics/parliament-approves-afcfta-secretariat-agreement.html)). A claim that the 2021 budget was never released appears in a summary I could not attribute, so I do not rely on it. Mene was reappointed in February 2024 for four more years ([Ghana News Agency](https://gna.org.gh/2024/02/mene-gets-the-nod-for-a-second-term-as-afcfta-secretary-general/)). My judgment is that the steps that actually gate trade (gazettal, customs systems, rule-of-origin administration, ratification) sit with national governments, so Secretariat capacity limits monitoring and technical support more than it limits outcomes; the World Bank's governance observation points the same way.

## Governments agree on implementation and split over who pays and who is bound

Priorities have converged on one word. The 39th AU Assembly (Addis Ababa, 14-15 February 2026) put AfCFTA delivery at the centre of its agenda, inaugurated a Committee of Heads of State and Government on implementation chaired by Kenya's William Ruto with Botswana's President Boko as deputy, adopted the eight annexes to the Intellectual Property Protocol, directed the Council of Ministers to negotiate a Protocol on Industrial Policy and Development, and asked for ratification of the five Phase II protocols by end-2026, with annual progress reports from 2027 ([tralac](https://www.tralac.org/documents/resources/cfta/7495-au-assembly-decision-on-report-of-1st-meeting-of-assembly-committee-of-heads-of-state-and-government-implementation-afcfta-february-2026.html); [UCT Trade Talks Africa](https://commerce.uct.ac.za/school-public-governance/articles/2026-03-12-clone-trade-talks-africa-policy-progress-and-people-behind-afcfta-issue-8)). Nigeria took the Council of Ministers chair from Egypt at the 18th meeting in Abuja in late June 2026, and minister Jumoke Oduwole set four priorities: faster implementation, regional value chains, digital trade, and finance for women-led firms, youth entrepreneurs and small businesses ([ThisDay](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2026/06/30/fg-calls-for-faster-afcfta-implementation-to-boost-industrialisation-digital-trade/)). Nigerian press reports quote her as saying "Treaties don't create jobs, trucks do," though I could not verify the exact source. Mene names agriculture, pharmaceuticals, transport and logistics, and automotive manufacturing as priority sectors ([Business and Financial Times](https://thebftonline.com/2026/01/28/afcfta-shifts-from-policy-to-action/)), and trade-finance costs, transport infrastructure, logistics bottlenecks and restrictions on the movement of people as the main barriers ([MyJoyOnline](https://www.myjoyonline.com/afcfta-needs-efficient-payment-systems-borders-and-infrastructure-to-boost-intra-african-trade-wamkele-mene/)). The Agenda 2063 frame is the Second Ten-Year Implementation Plan for 2024-2033 ([NEPAD](https://www.nepad.org/agenda2063-report/agenda-2063-second-ten-year-implementation-plan)), so the relevant horizon is a plan inside Agenda 2063, not a successor to it. Afreximbank's June 2026 annual meetings were themed "Intra-African Trade and Industrialisation: Pathway to Economic Sovereignty" ([Afreximbank](https://www.afreximbank.com/afreximbank-to-hold-its-33rd-annual-meetings-in-el-alamein-egypt-from-21-24-june-2026/)), the AfDB convened an inaugural Integrate Africa Forum under "Made in Africa, Marketed in Africa" ([AfDB](https://www.afdb.org/en/news-and-events/events/african-development-bank-convene-inaugural-integrate-africa-forum-93520)), and business groups want the AfCFTA "communicated as a business proposition rather than a compliance burden" alongside a 24-point "Private Sector Bill of Rights" ([Business and Financial Times](https://thebftonline.com/2026/05/06/private-sector-takes-centre-stage-in-afcfta-implementation-push/)). Unions and informal-trader groups ask for a simplified trade regime, an end to border extortion and seats on national implementation committees ([The Standard](https://www.standardmedia.co.ke/national/article/2001558570/unions-demand-reforms-at-busia-border-as-traders-bear-cost-of-afcfta)).

Consensus is genuine on rules of origin as a finished business, on non-tariff barriers, customs and logistics, on payments and digital trade, on regional value chains in agro-processing, pharmaceuticals and autos, and on finance for women, youth and small firms. ECDPM's five priorities for AfCFTA-REC relations (integrated customs processes, mutual recognition of standards, clearer treatment of domestic taxes and fees, stronger barrier reporting and resolution, and attention to geography, infrastructure and security) overlap almost entirely with Mene's barrier list ([ECDPM](https://ecdpm.org/work/how-afcfta-regional-relations-can-support-continental-trade)). Free movement shows how cheap this consensus can be. Dangote called again for a visa-free Africa in April 2026 ([IOL](https://iol.co.za/business-report/companies/2026-04-24-dangote-reiterates-calls-for-visa-free-africa-as-afc-pushes-for-homegrown-capital-markets/)), yet the movement protocol has four ratifications of the 15 needed.

The first real disagreement concerns who adjusts, and how fast. Tariff liberalisation is back-loaded for LDCs and the G6, the sensitive and excluded baskets exist to protect revenue and industry, and the US$1 billion Adjustment Fund covers roughly a tenth of the estimated need. Fears that weaker markets will be flooded are documented in Nigerian manufacturers' 2021 "dumping ground" warnings ([Vanguard](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2021/04/afcfta-concern-over-dumping-mounts-as-trade-deficit-widens/)) and in older ISS commentary on income disparities among members ([ISS African Futures](https://futures.issafrica.org/thematic/08-afcfta/)). Modelling cuts both ways: a Tripartite simulation found fears of concentration in Egypt and South Africa "exaggerated" ([Journal of African Trade](https://atlantis-press.com/journals/jat/125905673)), while UNCTAD finds that gains accrue first to economies with large manufacturing capacity ([UNCTAD](https://unctad.org/publication/african-continental-free-trade-area-challenges-and-opportunities-tariff-reductions)). The rules-of-origin fight shows how such conflicts get resolved. West African cotton producers split from garment exporters (Egypt, Ethiopia, Morocco), and fragile-industry states split from emerging auto exporters (Morocco, South Africa, Rwanda) ([Financial Afrik](https://www.financialafrik.com/en/2025/09/06/afcfta-rules-of-origin-facing-obstacles-in-the-automotive-and-textile-sectors/)); the Secretary-General asked for political guidance at the September 2025 ministerial retreat ([AfCFTA Secretariat](https://au-afcfta.org/2025/09/afcfta-secretary-general-calls-for-political-guidance-on-rules-of-origin-at-4th-ministerial-retreat/)), and consensus arrived through a transitional mechanism ([Arab Finance](https://arabfinance.com/en/news/newdetails/Egypt-secures-African-consensus-on-outstanding-AfCFTA-rules-of-origin)) after more than four years.

The second disagreement is whether treaty rules should bind industrial policy. The February 2026 mandate opens it. A Policy Center for the New South paper of 21 September 2026 proposes a definition narrow enough for treaty language but broad enough to cover real industrialisation instruments, built on shared principles, a "safe-harbour" catalogue of permitted instruments, targeted positive harmonisation and coordination mechanisms ([Policy Center for the New South](https://www.policycenter.ma/publications/how-negotiate-industrial-policy-under-afcfta-architecture-coherence-and-governance)). I found no national negotiating positions. My judgment, which rests on public framing rather than any reported negotiating position, is that the protocol will land on safe harbours rather than hard bans on subsidies or local-content rules: Afreximbank's "economic sovereignty" theme and Dangote's call to "keep capital at home" ([Newswire](https://www.newswire.ca/news-releases/dangote-calls-on-africa-to-keep-its-capital-at-home-as-industrialization-takes-center-stage-at-unstoppable-africa-832673936.html)) signal that industrialisers want room to act, and a binding ban would require those same governments to give it up.

The third disagreement is about primacy and sequencing between continent and region. The ECOWAS Parliament's Speaker says ECOWAS "cannot just accompany" the AfCFTA but should "lead, coordinate and harmonize" it ([Guardian Nigeria](https://guardian.ng/business-services/extraordinary-session-2026-stakeholders-move-to-strengthen-afcfta-implementation/)), while Article 19(2) lets members with deeper REC integration keep it ([tralac](https://www.tralac.org/blog/article/15746-the-role-of-negotiating-principles-in-the-design-of-the-afcfta.html)) and customs unions submitted bloc-wide tariff offers. That makes ECOWAS's fracture an AfCFTA problem: the AES discussed its own common external tariff and customs code in Bamako in October 2025 ([Habari Network](https://www.thehabarinetwork.com/alliance-of-sahel-states-moves-toward-a-customs-union-a-new-model-for-african-economic-integration)) and remains an AfCFTA member ([BusinessDay Nigeria](https://businessday.ng/maritime/article/sahel-blocs-import-tariff-on-ecowas-threatens-west-africa-trade-afcfta/)). Whether AES states are inside ECOWAS's bloc tariff offer is unverified. On deeper union I found no documented camp arguing for a continental customs union or currency before the AfCFTA works; the evidence is that Abuja's customs-union and common-market dates passed unmet, the EAC currency date moved from 2024 to 2031, and the AES currency has not materialised. Treating "deepen the AfCFTA first" as the working consensus is my inference.

The fourth is external. AGOA lapsed on 30 September 2025 ([FPRI](https://www.fpri.org/article/2025/10/the-african-growth-and-opportunity-act-is-no-more/)); the House passed an extension on 12 January 2026 (340-54), a one-year retroactive renewal through 31 December 2026 was signed on 3 February, and a further extension to 31 December 2028 was signed on 2 September 2026 after Senate (90-6) and House (370-48) votes ([Congress.gov](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/house-bill/6500); [Semafor](https://www.semafor.com/article/09/02/2026/us-africa-trade-pact-extended)); summaries disagree on "two" versus "three" years and on the Senate vote date, so the exact dates need verification. CSIS's Oge Onubogu says the extension "does not provide the certainty" African businesses need ([EWN](https://www.ewn.co.za/2026/09/09/america-extends-agoa-but-sa-investment-uncertainty-remains)). China applied zero tariffs on all lines from 53 African countries from 1 May 2026, excluding Eswatini ([Ecofin](https://www.ecofinagency.com/news/1602-52922-china-to-scrap-tariffs-on-imports-from-53-african-partners-from-may-1)). Kenya concluded an "Early Harvest" arrangement with China on 19 December 2025 while pursuing AGOA and a bilateral US deal, and says there is "no tension" ([The Standard](https://thestandard.ke/business/amp/financial-standard/article/2001538897/how-kenya-outwitted-us-china-to-clinch-dual-trade-deals)). ISS argued in August 2025 that African economies are "competitive and not complementary", that the AfCFTA can replace a lost US market only over the medium to long term, and that states competing for US deals by offering minerals could stall it ([ISS](https://issafrica.org/iss-today/why-the-afcfta-can-t-simply-plug-the-us-trade-gap)). The US tariff regime after the Section 122 tariffs lapsed on 24 July 2026 is not in my sources. I found nothing on how EPAs layer onto AfCFTA schedules and only an unverified aggregator report that an enhanced EU-Eastern and Southern Africa EPA was concluded in June 2026 ([Rio Times](https://www.riotimesonline.com/eu-africa-trade-deal-indian-ocean-epa-2026/)); FDI was not researched. My judgment is that AGOA-exposed exporters (the sources point to Lesotho, Madagascar, Kenya, South Africa) will treat bilateral US access as the priority through 2028 and will not trade it for deeper AfCFTA concessions; this rests on sector exposure, not on stated positions.

A last dispute is about financing credibility. Afreximbank runs PAPSS, the Adjustment Fund, trade-finance lines and the Intra-African Trade Fair (next in Lagos, 5-11 November 2027 ([Afreximbank](https://www.afreximbank.com/nigeria-to-host-the-intra-african-trade-fair-in-2027/))), yet it has been in a preferred-creditor dispute over the Ghana and Zambia restructurings and cut ties with Fitch after a downgrade to junk in January 2026 ([BusinessDay South Africa](https://www.businessday.co.za/world/2026-01-25-rating-cut-spurs-afreximbank-to-cut-ties-with-fitch/)); a June 2026 Polity headline indicates that S&P restored its investment-grade rating, but I saw only the headline ([Polity](https://polity.org.za/article/sp-restores-afreximbanks-investment-grade-rating-2026-06-12)). I read this as concentration risk: much of the implementation machinery depends on one institution whose standing with creditors is contested.

## Facilitation gains are shared, adjustment costs are concentrated, and that shapes what governments can agree

The best-evidenced opportunity and the main obstacle are the same variable: national administrative capacity. Facilitation carries about 65% of the World Bank's US$450 billion and sits inside customs, border and standards agencies, and the World Bank's August 2026 behind-the-border finding points the same way, though it comes from the same institution. Services liberalisation could raise AfCFTA services trade by about 60-64% by 2035 ([World Bank](https://www.worldbank.org/en/brief/2026/08/25/integrating-africa-from-threads-to-hubs)), though only 24 draft services schedules are adopted. Regional value chains are the most concrete industrial prize. The DRC and Zambia have a 2022 cooperation agreement for a transboundary battery zone and a precursor plant targeting 100,000 tonnes a year, and the DRC cabinet approved the Musompo zone (900 hectares, over US$200 million, about US$2 billion of private investment sought) in February 2026 ([DevelopmentAid](https://www.developmentaid.org/news-stream/post/206842/africas-high-stakes-mineral-gambit)); the same DRC imposes unilateral cobalt quotas, so a bilateral anchor deal and an AfCFTA-blind export-control regime coexist. Digital trade is the nearest protocol: its annexes are adopted, Nigeria's parliament approved ratification in November 2025 ([Digital Policy Alert](https://digitalpolicyalert.org/event/35530-nigeria-approved-ratification-of-protocol-to-agreement-establishing-african-continental-free-trade-area-on-digital-trade)), and the Secretariat cites a projected US$712 billion digital economy by 2050 ([AfCFTA Secretariat](https://au-afcfta.org/2026/07/afcfta-digital-trade-forum-closes-in-lagos-with-eight-calls-to-action-on-digital-market-implementation/)), a projection, not an outcome. Power and air links are early: SAPP traded 7.7 TWh in 2023 against about 344 TWh of demand, about 2% ([Energy Chamber](https://energychamber.org/african-power-pools-how-regional-integration-can-strengthen-energy-security/)). No source I retrieved shows that the AfCFTA has changed investment decisions.

I rank the obstacles in three tiers, a judgment built on the sourced evidence. Binding across the continent are non-tariff barriers and customs administration, logistics and infrastructure, commodity-heavy and similar production structures, and payment frictions. Binding for particular coalitions are tariff-revenue dependence and fiscal stress. Amplifiers are US tariffs and AGOA's short renewals, China's zero-tariff offer, the EU carbon border mechanism and the EU-Mercosur agreement provisionally applied since 1 May 2026 ([Finnish Customs](https://tulli.fi/en/-/free-trade-agreement-between-the-eu-and-mercosur-countries-to-be-applied-provisionally-from-1-may-2026-note-the-rules-of-origin)); tariffs themselves are the lesser friction. The revenue evidence shows why the middle tier matters. The IMF puts losses at 0.03-0.22% of GDP ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/Staff-Discussion-Notes/Issues/2020/05/13/The-African-Continental-Free-Trade-Area-Potential-Economic-Impact-and-Challenges-46235)), UNECA at about US$4.1 billion, or 9.1% of tariff revenue ([UNECA](https://archive.uneca.org/node/16065)), and a September 2025 IGC working paper finds losses insignificant ([IGC](https://www.theigc.org/sites/default/files/2025-12/Edwards-Chien-Kumwenda-Hakizimzna-Rwagasana-Working-Paper-September-2025%20.pdf)). Yet Togo's customs revenue loss over three years is put at 22.67% of customs revenue ([JUTA Journals](https://www.jutajournals.co.za/impact-of-the-afcfta-agreement-on-customs-revenue-case-of-togo)), Botswana, Namibia, Eswatini and Lesotho draw 28-40% of government revenue from the SACU pool (Namibia 32.6% in FY2023/24) ([The Brief Namibia](https://thebrief.com.na/2023/02/namibia-warned-over-sacu-revenue-over-reliance/)), and trade taxes are about 34% of revenue in African LDCs, with the year unclear ([Afronomics Law](https://www.afronomicslaw.org/2020/04/14/preserving-africas-fiscal-sustainability-in-the-wake-of-the-africa-continental-free-trade-area-agreement)). The aggregate is small and the exposure is concentrated, which makes the IMF's "limited" conclusion understate the salience for the finance ministries that decide gazettal; that is my inference.

Fiscal space is shrinking at the same time: the IMF puts Sub-Saharan growth at 4.5% in 2025 and 4.3% in 2026, citing the Middle East war and a "sharp, likely structural, decline in foreign aid" ([IMF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/reo/ssa/issues/2026/04/16/regional-economic-outlook-for-sub-saharan-africa-april-2026)), and the OECD reports aid down 23.1% in 2025, bilateral aid to Sub-Saharan Africa down 26.3% to US$29.2 billion, and a further 11.6% fall projected for 2026 ([OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2026/04/international-aid-fell-sharply-in-2025-says-oecd.html)). Debt-distress tallies in my sources conflict (from 19 of 35 low-income countries to "over 32 African countries") and I do not quote one. For the EU carbon border mechanism, whose definitive phase began on 1 January 2026, models cited in African press put Africa's GDP loss at about 0.91% (roughly US$25 billion), with aluminium most exposed and aluminium about 68% of Mozambique's 2024 exports ([African Business](https://african.business/2025/11/politics/africa-braces-for-impact-of-eu-carbon-border-tax); [LSE](https://www.lse.ac.uk/news/latest-news-from-lse/e-may-2023/africa-could-lose-up-to-25-billion-per-annum-as-a-direct-result-of-the-eus-cbam)), though the attribution among studies is approximate.

These obstacles matter politically because each maps onto an identifiable interest. Border officials who earn from discretion lose from facilitation; UN Women documents harassment, bribe solicitation and sexual exploitation of women traders, who are about 70% of informal cross-border traders ([Nation Africa](https://nation.africa/kenya/news/gender/lobby-calls-for-gender-responsive-trade-policies-4131960)). Finance ministries lose revenue, import-competing producers lose protection (Tanzania's 25% duty on Kenyan confectionery, imposed on a claim that zero-rated sugar was misused ([The Citizen](https://www.thecitizen.co.tz/news/1840340-5150218-8r5fn1/index.html))), West African cotton growers and Egyptian or Ethiopian garment makers want opposite rules, and the EAC's non-tariff barriers (weighbridges, police roadblocks, tax measures) persist two decades after its 2005 customs union ([The Exchange Africa](https://theexchange.africa/tanzania-kenya-eac-trade-investment-regulations/)). Regime security enters too: analysts link the AES exit partly to escaping pressure to hold elections ([Amani Africa](https://amaniafrica-et.org/the-withdr)), which shows that a bloc can lose members when integration collides with regime preferences. Hegemons are both likeliest winners and targets of suspicion, and the May 2026 South Africa episode and Nigeria's 2019 closure show they are unreliable champions of openness when domestic politics turn. **The decisive asymmetry is that the gains are diffuse and delayed while the costs are concentrated and early, and the people who must implement the reforms partly lose from them.** That predicts slow, selective implementation even when heads of state endorse the project, on the assumption that the 65% facilitation share of modelled gains is roughly right.

The same asymmetry sorts what governments can agree. The system has resolved stalemates in four ways: deadlines plus political guidance, transitional compromises (the rules of origin), coalitions of the willing (the Guided Trade Initiative) and side funds (the Adjustment Fund). Facilitation priorities (single windows, mutual recognition, barrier resolution, payments) can pass through all four and are already endorsed by every actor above, though endorsing them is cheaper than delivering them. Contested items (investment and competition protocols, industrial policy, any customs union) touch sovereignty, state aid and revenue, and the tools above fit them poorly. The documented ratification evidence supports that reading. No source gives a count for any Phase II protocol; the Digital Trade Protocol's 22-ratification threshold was not met as of mid-2026 ([Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/international-trade-investment/1816936/one-africa-one-click-what-the-afcfta-digital-trade-protocol-means-for-africa)), the July 2026 Council still listed an outstanding Investment annex and an Intellectual Property annex ([AfCFTA Secretariat](https://au-afcfta.org/2026/07/afcfta-council-of-ministers-concludes-18th-meeting-in-abuja-nigeria-assumes-the-chairpersonship-as-focus-shifts-fully-to-implementation/)), and the only documented movers are Nigeria on digital trade and South Africa, which concluded domestic processes for the Competition and Women and Youth protocols ([dtic](https://www.thedtic.gov.za/wp-content/uploads/Newsletter-on-the-AfCFTA-March-2026.pdf)). **I expect the end-2026 ratification target to be missed for most protocols and the Digital Trade Protocol to be the first to enter into force, probably in 2027.** That rests on two assumptions: that legislatures wait for complete texts, and that ministries will not spend political capital on investment and data-flow constraints for uncertain gains while fiscal space contracts.

Comparative experience supports a facilitation-first sequence and doubts a continental customs union this decade. Intra-ASEAN trade rose only from 23% (2000) to 25.3% (2014) of members' trade despite tariff cuts, with non-tariff measures the main remaining hurdle ([PIDS](https://www.pids.gov.ph/details/increasing-nontariff-measures-limit-ph-capacity-to-trade-in-asean-region-pids-study)). The EAC's asymmetric design (Kenya cut internal tariffs at once in 2005 while its goods faced tariffs elsewhere until December 2009) did not stop non-tariff barriers ([Capital FM](https://capitalfm.africa/a-fully-fledged-custom-union-in-place/)). The ECOWAS common external tariff took about ten years to negotiate among 15 members and was formally adopted in October 2013 ([FERDI](https://ferdi.fr/dl/df-b4ZPYtVWGy14VkWXVkgkZsZg/ferdi-b107-reflections-on-adapting-to-the-ecowas-cet.pdf)). SACU's revenue formula shows compensation can hold a customs union together, at the price of dependence and a payer, South Africa at about 97% of the pool, pressing for review ([SACU factsheet](https://www.sacu.int/uploads/documents/SACU%20FACTSHEET%20UNDERSTANDING%20THE%20SACU%20REVENUE%20SHARING%20ARRANGEMENT%205_9-211024-095719.pdf); [BusinessDay](https://www.businessday.co.za/bd/opinion/2025-07-01-henni-britz-time-to-review-sacus-revenue-sharing-formula/)). A continental version lacks a single willing payer, which is why I judge the Adjustment Fund, targeted at small and LDC revenue-dependent states, the more plausible route. Mercosur's expanding exception lists, with Brazil's and Argentina's allowance rising from 100 to 150 items by 2028 ([Datamar News](https://datamarnews.com/?p=62415)), and the US decision at the 1 July 2026 USMCA review not to extend the agreement, triggering annual reviews ([White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/usmca-2026-joint-review-united-states-declines-extend-agreement-triggering-annual)), show external-tariff and rules-of-origin policy turning into leverage when a bloc contains a dominant market. **The defensible 2026-2030 order is facilitation and payments first, protocols and industrial policy second, and any customs union a distant third, with a "customs-union-lite" (common standards, mutual recognition, shared customs data) as the realistic step.** The sources on the EU, ASEAN, Mercosur and USMCA are summaries, and the EU's cohesion funds, CPTPP design and the Mercosur crises were not researched.

## Conclusion

What the evidence lacks is a measurement, not a policy: customs-based statistics on preferential imports by corridor and by state pair. Without them, the roughly 19% upper bound on live country pairs cannot be converted into a share of trade, and the optimistic projections (US$250 billion in 2026) and the sceptical readings (trade "increased only slightly") both extrapolate from series whose revisions are larger than their movements. The first useful datum is therefore not a new protocol but the next Afreximbank edition, which will show whether the 2025 baseline survives a second revision, and a published count of gazetted schedules that states how SACU is counted.

The decision points fall within about two years: the end-2026 Phase II ratification target and the December 2026 end of ECOWAS's mandate to negotiate with the AES, then the "eco" in 2027, the first annual Phase II progress reports in 2027, the Lagos trade fair in November 2027, Mene's term ending in early 2028 on a four-year reading of his February 2024 reappointment, and AGOA's expiry on 31 December 2028. On the event record, the agenda runs through a few states and one lender (Kenya's Ruto chairs the implementation committee, Nigeria chairs the Council and hosts the trade fair, Egypt hosted the 2026 AU mid-year and Afreximbank meetings, and Afreximbank finances payments, adjustment and trade finance). The implication is that the AfCFTA's near-term credibility depends less on negotiating new texts than on whether this small group converts endorsement into gazettal, customs reform and ratification. The Heads of State committee gives the project political oversight that no REC had, but with no sanction and no dispute-settlement case in my sources, compliance still rests on peer pressure.
